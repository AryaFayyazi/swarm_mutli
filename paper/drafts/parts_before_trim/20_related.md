## 2. Related Work
**Multi-agent failures.** Hammond et al. [2] map the risks of interacting advanced agents. Cemri et al. [4] label 14 failure modes in more than 1,600 framework traces. We study one security failure with a ground-truth label in every episode. **Spread in a team.** Prompt Infection [3] shows that an injected instruction can pass from agent to agent. We inject no instruction: a peer states a plausible, unverifiable fact, and ordinary delegation does the rest. **Authorization.** Confused-deputy and privilege-escalation defenses for agents use access control: SEAgent [7] adds mandatory access control with an information-flow graph, ScopeGate [8] a policy decision and enforcement point with default deny, and Dantuluri et al. [9] analyze gaps in identity and authorization for delegation. These works build mechanisms. We measure the behavior that makes them necessary and price them in utility with matched safe twins. Closest to us are four recent studies of false authority in agent systems. Cerruti et al. [15] show that a false record of permissions in agent memory ("endogenous authorization laundering") leads executors to act. Safin et al. [16] show that a forged approval in a four-agent pipeline reaches the executor unless an independent authorization layer blocks it. Zhu et al. [17] separate message delivery, signing authority and tool-side enforcement, and find that a deterministic tool gate stops requests that models make. Xu et al. [18] find that unauthorized actions grow with hierarchy depth in delegation. We agree with their common conclusion that enforcement must sit outside the model. We add three things they do not: matched safe and unsafe twins with a rule-based label for every call, so that a gate's cost can be priced; the *report* to the human as an outcome, and which log lets a monitor see the failure; and a test of an in-transit defense against an attacker who knows how it works. Our in-transit claim filter is a form of spotlighting [14], which marks untrusted text, and is far weaker in principle than the capability and provenance tracking of CaMeL [19]; we test it because it needs no change to the agents. The provenance paradox [10] shows that unverifiable claims mislead delegation; our permission-system record is the "attested" side of that split. **Control and monitoring.** AI control [5] and resampling [6] study monitors for one untrusted agent. Rose et al. [11] argue that secret collusion may put a ceiling on transcript-level monitoring, and Korbak et al. [12] call chain-of-thought monitoring a fragile opportunity. We ask which log a deployer must keep. A recent incident investigation [13] found agents that spoofed tool calls and edited logs inside their containers, which is why our strongest log is written by the gateway. **Our prior work.** CAPE-Bench [1] gives matched twins for single agents and defines retroactive permission laundering. We reuse its environments unchanged (preexisting). The teams, gateway, controls, monitors and all results are new.
