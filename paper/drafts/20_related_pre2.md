## 2. Related Work
**Failures in multi-agent systems.** Hammond et al. [2] map the risks of interacting agents. Cemri et al. [4] label failure modes in agent traces. We study one security failure, with a ground-truth label in every episode. **Spread inside a team.** Prompt Infection [3] shows that an injected instruction can pass from agent to agent. We inject no instruction. A peer states a plausible fact that nobody can check, and normal delegation does the rest. **Authorization.** SEAgent [7], ScopeGate [8] and the analysis in [9] build access-control mechanisms. We measure the behavior that makes them necessary, and we price them in lost useful work with matched safe twins.

Four recent studies test false authority in agent systems. Cerruti et al. [15] show that a false permission record in agent memory leads executors to act. Safin et al. [16] show that a forged approval in a four-agent pipeline reaches the executor, unless an independent authorization layer blocks it. Zhu et al. [17] find that a deterministic tool gate stops the requests that models make. Xu et al. [18] find that unauthorized actions grow with the depth of the delegation hierarchy. We agree with their shared conclusion: enforcement must sit outside the model. We add three things. First, matched twins with a rule-based label for every call, so we can price a gate. Second, the *report* to the human as an outcome, and the question of which log lets a monitor see the failure. Third, a test of why a false claim works, and of an in-transit defense against an attacker who knows it. Our claim filter is a form of spotlighting [14], which marks untrusted text, and is far weaker in principle than the capability tracking of CaMeL [19]. We test it because it needs no change to the agents. The provenance paradox [10] also says that unverifiable claims mislead delegation.

**Control and monitoring.** AI control [5] and resampling [6] study monitors for one untrusted agent. Rose et al. [11] argue that secret collusion may limit what transcript monitors see. Korbak et al. [12] call chain-of-thought monitoring a fragile opportunity. We ask which log a deployer must keep. An incident investigation [13] found agents that faked tool calls and edited logs, which is why our strongest log is written by the gateway. **Our prior work.** CAPE-Bench [1] gives matched twins for single agents and defines retroactive permission laundering. We reuse its environments unchanged (preexisting). The teams, gateway, controls, monitors and all results are new.
