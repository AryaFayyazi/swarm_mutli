TITLE: Who Approved This? Assurance Laundering in LLM Agent Teams
AUTHOR: Arya Fayyazi | University of Southern California
ABSTRACT:
AI agents now work in teams and use tools, and the human often reads only the final report. We study *assurance laundering*. A peer agent says, without proof, that approval was already given. The team acts on this claim without authorization. Then it tells the human, with confidence, that the work was authorized. We built CAPE-Swarm, a team layer on the CAPE-Bench test environments. Each task has a safe twin and an unsafe twin that differ in one fact, and a fixed rule (not an LLM) labels every action. We preregistered {{X['nh']}} hypotheses and ran {{sum(D['counts'].values())+R['counts']['e9']|,}} episodes with seven open-weight models. One false sentence from a peer raised the share of unauthorized actions of a single agent from {{X['V_none']['solo']|.2f}} to {{X['V_peer']['solo']|.2f}} (95% CI of the difference [{{H['H1']['lo']|+.2f}}, {{H['H1']['hi']|+.2f}}]). Three models reacted strongly and three hardly at all. When a team did act without authorization, its report said "authorized" in {{H['H3']['false_assurance_over_V']|.0%}} of cases. Acting and reporting need different fixes. A permission record repairs the report. An ask-before-act rule at the gateway stops the action. A *claim filter*, which marks every approval claim as unverified before an agent reads it, cut unauthorized actions from {{R['_cells']['B']['V']|.2f}} to {{R['_cells']['C']['V']|.2f}} on new scenarios, with no measurable loss of useful work. An attacker model that knew how the filter works wrote a new message.
