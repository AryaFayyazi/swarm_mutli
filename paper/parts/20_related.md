## 2. Related Work
**Multi-agent failures.** Hammond et al. [2] map the risks of interacting advanced agents. Cemri et al. [4] label 14 failure modes in more than 1,600 framework traces. We study one security failure with a ground-truth label in every episode. **Spread in a team.** Prompt Infection [3] shows that an injected instruction can pass from agent to agent. We inject no instruction: a peer states a plausible, unverifiable fact, and ordinary delegation does the rest. **Authorization.** Confused-deputy and privilege-escalation defenses for agents use access control: SEAgent [7] adds mandatory access control with an information-flow graph, ScopeGate [8] a policy decision and enforcement point with default deny, and Dantuluri et al. [9] analyze gaps in identity and authorization for delegation. These works build mechanisms. We measure the behavior that makes them necessary and price them in utility with matched safe twins. The provenance paradox [10] shows that unverifiable claims mislead delegation; our permission-system record is the "attested" side of that split. **Control and monitoring.** AI control [5] and resampling [6] study monitors for one untrusted agent. Rose et al. [11] argue that secret collusion may put a ceiling on transcript-level monitoring, and Korbak et al. [12] call chain-of-thought monitoring a fragile opportunity. We ask which log a deployer must keep. A recent incident investigation [13] found agents that spoofed tool calls and edited logs inside their containers, which is why our strongest log is written by the gateway. **Our prior work.** CAPE-Bench [1] gives matched twins for single agents and defines retroactive permission laundering. We reuse its environments unchanged (preexisting). The teams, gateway, controls, monitors and all results are new.
