## 2. Related Work
**Failures in multi-agent systems.** Hammond et al. [2] and Cemri et al. [4] map the risks and failure modes of agent teams. We study one security failure, with a ground-truth label in every episode. Prompt Infection [3] shows that an injected instruction can pass from agent to agent. We inject no instruction: a peer states a plausible fact that nobody can check, and normal delegation does the rest. Access-control mechanisms such as [7–9] are what such a failure calls for. We measure the behavior that makes them necessary, and we price them in lost useful work.

**False authority in agent systems.** Four recent studies are closest. Cerruti et al. [15] show that a false permission record in agent memory leads executors to act. Safin et al. [16] show that a forged approval reaches the executor of a four-agent pipeline unless an independent authorization layer blocks it. Zhu et al. [17] find that a deterministic tool gate stops the requests that models make. Xu et al. [18] find that unauthorized actions grow with the depth of the delegation hierarchy. We agree that enforcement must sit outside the model, and we add three things: matched twins with a rule-based label for every call, so that we can price a gate; the *report* to the human as an outcome, with the question of which log shows the failure; and a test of why the false claim works and of an in-transit defense against an attacker who knows it. Our claim filter is a form of spotlighting [14], which marks untrusted text, and is far weaker in principle than the capability tracking of CaMeL [19]. We test it because it needs no change to the agents.

**Control and monitoring.** AI control [5] and resampling [6] study monitors for one untrusted agent. Rose et al. [11] and Korbak et al. [12] discuss what transcript and reasoning monitors can see. We ask which log a deployer must keep. An incident investigation [13] found agents that faked tool calls and edited logs, which is why our strongest log is written by the gateway. **Our prior work.** CAPE-Bench [1] gives matched twins for single agents and defines retroactive permission laundering. We reuse its environments unchanged (preexisting). The teams, gateway, controls, monitors and all results are new.
